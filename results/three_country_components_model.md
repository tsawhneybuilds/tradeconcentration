# Three-Country Components Model: From Components To Corridors

Generated: 2026-05-29

## Purpose

This memo develops a first-pass model for the project hypothesis that trade concentration is generated by scalable product-destination-source corridors, not by products or partners alone. The model is designed to explain the Panagariya-Bagaria puzzle: countries trade many products, but trade value is concentrated in a small number of products, partners, and bilateral product cells; imports can be nearly as concentrated as exports; and broad component stories only partially explain the fact.

The model is deliberately kept at the level needed for a theory section and empirical design. It is not yet a calibrated quantitative trade model.

## Empirical Facts To Discipline The Model

Use these as target moments rather than background motivation.

1. Concentration is real relative to sparse-data benchmarks. In the `rd2_countries` HS2-preserving benchmark, median product Gini is far above the simulated median for both exports and imports.
2. Product-partner-cell growth is central. In the latest HS6-family extensive-margin decomposition, median 5-year net-growth shares are about `0.424` for net-new products, `0.002` for net-new partners for existing products, `0.160` for new product-partner cells among existing products and partners, `1.037` for growth in already-active cells, and `-0.765` for contraction in already-active cells.
3. Broad import Product Gini is not an export-input mechanism by itself. Exercise 11 finds that HS6 and HS2 import concentration contributors are weakly or negatively linked to same-product or same-chapter exports.
4. Import source relationships are sticky. Exercise 13 finds a same-top-supplier rate around `0.690` and value-weighted same-top-supplier rate around `0.849`.
5. Source concentration is usually economy-specific, not pure global dominance. In Exercise 13, `economy_specific` import-source concentration is much larger than `global_dominant` concentration in the median latest country.
6. Intermediates are large by value, but energy and domestic-demand products can also raise import concentration. The model must allow import concentration unrelated to export production.

## Related Literatures And Gap

- Panagariya and Bagaria document national product and partner concentration and explicitly argue that a full explanation likely needs at least three countries, components, and heterogeneous firms.
- Antras, Fort, and Tintelnot model multi-country sourcing with firm self-selection and complementarities across source markets.
- Helpman, Melitz, and Rubinstein plus Eaton, Kortum, and Kramarz explain destination selection, zero trade flows, and firm participation across markets.
- Bernard, Redding, and Schott explain multi-product firms and within-firm product selection.
- Johnson and Noguera, Hummels-Ishii-Yi, and Yi discipline production-sharing and vertical-specialization measurement.

The gap is the joint concentration object. Existing models explain sourcing, exporting, product scope, or production sharing. This project asks why national trade value is concentrated across products, partners, and product-source/product-destination cells, and why broad import concentration can fail to map to export production.

## Environment

There is a set of countries `n in N`. For exposition, focus on a triad:

- `A`: assembler/exporter country.
- `S`: component-source country.
- `D`: final-demand country.

The formal environment keeps many possible sources `s in N` and destinations `d in N`, because a literal three-country model cannot generate meaningful partner concentration.

There is a continuum of firms `f` in country `A`. Firms draw:

```text
firm productivity:        phi_f
product capability:       a_fj for each final product j
source match quality:     eta_fms for component m from source s
```

Final products are indexed by `j`. Component or input products are indexed by `m`. A technology matrix `omega_jm >= 0` maps final product `j` to its component requirements. This is the key measurement feature: the imported component HS6 code `m` need not equal the exported final HS6 code `j`.

## Demand

Destination `d` has CES demand for varieties of final product `j`. A firm from `A` selling product `j` to destination `d` earns revenue

```text
r_fjd = B_jd * (tau_Ad * c_fj)^(-[sigma_j - 1])
```

where:

- `B_jd` is destination-product demand.
- `tau_Ad >= 1` is the iceberg export trade cost from `A` to `d`.
- `c_fj` is the firm's marginal cost for product `j`.
- `sigma_j > 1` is the elasticity of substitution.

With constant-markup monopolistic competition, operating profit is

```text
op_fjd = r_fjd / sigma_j
```

## Production And Imported Inputs

Firm `f` produces final product `j` with labour and a bundle of components. The marginal cost is

```text
c_fj(S_fj) =
    (w_A / [phi_f * a_fj])^(1 - alpha_j)
    * P_Mj(S_fj)^(alpha_j)
```

where `S_fj` is the set of component-source pairs chosen by firm `f` for product `j`, and `alpha_j in [0,1]` is input intensity.

The imported-input price index for product `j` is

```text
P_Mj(S_fj) =
    [ sum_m omega_jm * P_fm(S_fm)^(1 - rho_j) ]^(1 / [1 - rho_j])
```

For each component `m`, the source price index is

```text
P_fm(S_fm) =
    [ sum_{s in S_fm} (tau_sA * p_ms / eta_fms)^(-theta_m) ]^(-1 / theta_m)
```

where:

- `p_ms` is the source-country production cost for component `m`.
- `tau_sA` is the source-to-assembler trade cost.
- `eta_fms` is firm-source match quality.
- `theta_m` controls substitutability or dispersion across source options.

Adding a source can lower the input price index, but it requires a fixed relationship cost.

## Fixed Costs

The firm faces four fixed costs:

```text
F_j^P       product-scope fixed cost for producing/exporting product j
F_d^D       destination access fixed cost
F_jd^X      product-destination fixed cost
F_ms^M      component-source relationship fixed cost
```

The `F_ms^M` term bundles search, compliance, standards, logistics setup, payment systems, trust, certification, and policy frictions. In a dynamic version it becomes a sunk cost plus switching cost, which generates persistence.

## Firm Problem

For each product-destination pair `(j,d)`, the firm chooses source sets and export participation:

```text
max_{S_fj, x_fjd in {0,1}}
    sum_d x_fjd * [ r_fjd(S_fj) / sigma_j - F_jd^X - F_d^D ]
    - 1{sum_d x_fjd > 0} * F_j^P
    - sum_{m,s in S_fj} F_ms^M
```

The firm exports product `j` to destination `d` if optimized operating profit covers the relevant fixed costs.

## Aggregation

Country `A` exports of final product `j` to destination `d` are

```text
X_Ajd = sum_f x_fjd * r_fjd
```

Product-destination cells are the aggregate counterpart of firm-level participation.

Country `A` imports component `m` from source `s` for export production are

```text
M_Ams^prod = sum_f sum_j sum_d input_value_fjmsd
```

Total imports also include domestic final demand, energy, investment, and non-export-linked demand:

```text
M_Ams = M_Ams^prod + M_Ams^domestic
```

This term is essential. It lets broad import Product Gini be high even when concentration-driving import products are not strongly export-linked.

## Equilibrium Concept

A partial-equilibrium corridor equilibrium consists of:

1. Firm choices of products, destinations, and source sets.
2. CES prices and revenues under constant markups.
3. Aggregate exports `X_Ajd` and imports `M_Ams`.
4. Country-level concentration measures computed from the aggregated product, partner, product-destination, and product-source distributions.

The first version takes wages, source costs, trade costs, and destination expenditure as primitives. A later GE extension would clear labour markets and determine wages and expenditure.

## Main Results To Derive

### Proposition 1: Export Participation Cutoffs

For fixed source set `S_fj`, product-destination operating profit is increasing in `phi_f * a_fj` and decreasing in `tau_Ad`, `F_jd^X`, and `F_d^D`. Therefore, each product-destination cell has a cutoff:

```text
phi_f * a_fj >= Phi_jd(S_fj)
```

Only firms above the cutoff serve the cell. Harder destinations and products with higher fixed costs have higher cutoffs.

Economic meaning: partner concentration arises because only some destinations clear the market-access threshold for many firms.

### Proposition 2: Product Concentration From Firm-Product Skewness

If `a_fj` is skewed across products within firms and fixed product costs are positive, aggregate product exports are concentrated even when firms are multi-product. High-productivity firms export several products, but most value is in their core products.

Economic meaning: "firms export many products" does not imply low product concentration. A firm can touch many HS codes while earning most export revenue from a narrow core.

### Proposition 3: Source Concentration From Relationship Fixed Costs

A firm adds component source `s` for component `m` only if the input-cost reduction exceeds `F_ms^M`. When relationship costs are high or match quality is skewed, most firms use a small number of sources, and import value concentrates in persistent source-product corridors.

Economic meaning: source concentration does not require one globally dominant supplier. It can arise from importer-specific relationships.

### Proposition 4: Product-Partner-Cell Entry Without New-Partner Entry

A decline in product-destination fixed costs `F_jd^X`, or an increase in firm capability `a_fj`, can create new product-partner cells among destinations already served by the country. New cells can therefore account for growth while new partners contribute little.

Economic meaning: this matches Exercise 12: the action is in product-partner-cell recombination and product entry, not in adding brand-new partner countries.

### Proposition 5: Broad Import Product Gini Need Not Predict Exports

If `M_Ams^domestic` is large or if the IO matrix `omega_jm` maps components `m` into final products `j != m`, then concentration-driving import HS6 products need not be exported in the same HS6 product or HS2 chapter. Broad import Product Gini can be weakly or negatively associated with same-product exports.

Economic meaning: the repo's negative Exercise 11 product-linkage result does not kill the corridor model. It kills the naive broad component interpretation.

### Proposition 6: Corridor-Specific Input Exposure Should Matter More Than Broad Product Gini

For input-intensive final products with high `alpha_j`, a fall in source costs or relationship costs for important components lowers `c_fj` and increases export revenue. The effect is strongest for firm-product-destination cells near the export cutoff.

Economic meaning: the next empirical test should use IO-weighted imported-input exposure, source concentration, and product-source corridors, not broad Product Gini alone.

## Formal Derivation Notes

### Cutoff Algebra

For a fixed source set `S_fj`, write marginal cost as

```text
c_fj =
    w_A^(1 - alpha_j)
    * P_Mj(S_fj)^alpha_j
    * (phi_f * a_fj)^(-(1 - alpha_j)).
```

Substituting into CES revenue gives

```text
r_fjd =
    B_jd
    * tau_Ad^(-(sigma_j - 1))
    * w_A^(-(1 - alpha_j)(sigma_j - 1))
    * P_Mj(S_fj)^(-alpha_j(sigma_j - 1))
    * (phi_f * a_fj)^((1 - alpha_j)(sigma_j - 1)).
```

Let the incremental fixed cost of serving cell `(j,d)` be

```text
Fbar_jd = F_jd^X + destination/product fixed-cost terms relevant at the margin.
```

The cell is active when `r_fjd / sigma_j >= Fbar_jd`. The implied cutoff is

```text
phi_f * a_fj >=
    [
        sigma_j * Fbar_jd
        * tau_Ad^(sigma_j - 1)
        * w_A^((1 - alpha_j)(sigma_j - 1))
        * P_Mj(S_fj)^(alpha_j(sigma_j - 1))
        / B_jd
    ] ^ [1 / ((1 - alpha_j)(sigma_j - 1))].
```

This cutoff delivers the central comparative statics:

- Higher destination demand `B_jd` lowers the cutoff.
- Higher export trade costs `tau_Ad` and fixed costs `Fbar_jd` raise the cutoff.
- Lower imported-input prices `P_Mj` lower the cutoff, especially when `alpha_j` is high.
- Higher firm productivity `phi_f` and product capability `a_fj` make cell entry more likely.

### Source-Relationship Rule

Firm `f` adds source `s` for component `m` used in product `j` if the gain in operating profit across active and potential destination cells exceeds the relationship cost:

```text
Delta_fjms =
    sum_d x_fjd *
        [ r_fjd(S_fj union {(m,s)}) - r_fjd(S_fj) ] / sigma_j
    - F_ms^M
    >= 0.
```

For a small reduction in the input bundle price, the operating-profit gain is approximately

```text
Delta op_fjd
    approx
    [(sigma_j - 1) * alpha_j / sigma_j]
    * r_fjd
    * [-Delta ln P_Mj].
```

This means source relationships are more likely for high-revenue firms, input-intensive products, components with large IO weights, and source matches that sharply reduce the input price index. The same formula also explains why source concentration can persist: once a relationship is paid for, incumbents have lower effective costs than potential switchers.

### Product-Partner Recombination

The model separates three entry margins:

```text
new product:                 country A did not export product j before
new partner:                 country A did not export anything to d before
new product-partner cell:     A exported j and exported to d before,
                              but not j to d
```

Exercise 12 shows the third margin is large while new-partner entry is tiny. In the model, this is natural when destination fixed costs `F_d^D` have already been paid by at least some firms, but product-destination fixed costs `F_jd^X` or firm-product capabilities still determine which products can enter already-served destinations.

## Minimal Two-Product Example

The model needs to explain why broad import concentration can be real but still fail as a same-product export mechanism.

Consider two imported products:

```text
m = C: a component used by export product j
m = E: energy/final-demand import not used directly in j
```

Suppose production imports of component `C` are valuable for export product `j`, but domestic energy imports `E` are much larger:

```text
M_AC^prod = 100,   M_AC^domestic = 0
M_AE^prod = 0,     M_AE^domestic = 900
```

Total import product concentration is then dominated by `E`, even though `C` is the export-relevant input. A regression of export value for product `j` on broad import Product Gini can therefore be null or negative. The correct model-implied object is not broad Product Gini; it is an IO-weighted source-corridor exposure:

```text
InputExposure_Aj =
    sum_m omega_jm * ImportedInputAccess_Am
```

where one possible source-access measure is

```text
ImportedInputAccess_Am =
    sum_s share_Ams * log(eta_Ams / [tau_sA * p_ms])
```

or, with only public aggregate data,

```text
SourceConcentration_Ajm =
    omega_jm * HHI_{sources}(M_Ams)
```

This is the empirical bridge from the model to the next test: use IO-linked inputs and source corridors, not exact HS6 import-export matches.

## Dynamic Extension

To match top-supplier persistence, let source relationships have a sunk setup cost and a lower continuation cost:

```text
first use of source s for component m:      K_ms + f_ms
continued use next period:                  f_ms
switch away and return later:               K_ms + f_ms
```

The state variable is the previous source set `S_fm,t-1`. The firm solves

```text
V_fjt(S_fm,t-1) =
    max_{S_fm,t, x_fjdt}
        current profit
        - sum_{m,s newly added} K_ms
        - sum_{m,s active} f_ms
        + beta E[V_fj,t+1(S_fm,t)].
```

This extension predicts:

- high same-top-supplier persistence even when current price rankings move;
- stronger persistence for high-certification or standards-heavy inputs;
- importer-specific dominance when relationship costs and match quality differ by importer;
- sharper export responses to source-cost shocks for firms already connected to the affected source.

## Comparative Statics

| Shock | Model prediction | Empirical object |
|---|---|---|
| Lower destination fixed cost `F_d^D` | More partner entry and lower partner concentration | Partner entry share, partner Gini |
| Lower product-destination fixed cost `F_jd^X` | More new product-partner cells inside existing partner network | Exercise 12 new-cell share |
| Lower product fixed cost `F_j^P` | More product entry and lower product concentration unless top firms scale more | New-product share, active products, product Gini |
| Lower source relationship cost `F_ms^M` | More source diversification and lower top-supplier persistence | Top supplier persistence, source HHI |
| Higher input intensity `alpha_j` | Imported-input cost shocks have larger export effects | IO-weighted exposure by sector |
| Higher source match dispersion `eta_fms` | More economy-specific source concentration | Exercise 13 ecosystem classes |
| Higher demand lump `M_Ams^domestic` | Import Product Gini rises without export linkage | Exercise 3 and Exercise 11 contrast |
| Higher firm-product skewness `a_fj` | Product concentration rises even with multi-product firms | Top product shares, EDD firm proxy |

## Empirical Mapping

The model implies a sequence of tests:

1. **Moment validation.** Report concentration by product, partner, product-partner cell, and product-source cell against random and HS2-preserving benchmarks.
2. **Entry decomposition.** Split export growth into new products, new partners, new cells among existing product/partner sets, continuing-cell growth, and continuing-cell contraction.
3. **Source persistence.** Decompose source concentration into global dominance, importer-specific dominance, and diffuse supply.
4. **Broad-input falsification.** Show that broad import Product Gini does not robustly predict same-product or same-chapter exports.
5. **Corridor-input test.** Test whether IO-weighted source concentration for required inputs predicts export-sector value, top-export-sector status, or new-cell survival.
6. **Gravity residualization.** Residualize source and destination concentration on GDP, distance, region, language, trade agreements, and tariffs. The model's relationship-cost channel is the residual economy-specific concentration.
7. **Survival.** New product-partner cells should survive and deepen more when they are attached to strong source corridors or high-capability product groups.

## What Would Make The Model Novel

The model is novel if it is positioned as a model of national trade concentration across three margins:

```text
product concentration
partner concentration
product-partner/source-corridor concentration
```

It is not novel as a generic heterogeneous-firm model, global sourcing model, gravity model, or vertical-specialization model. The contribution is combining these mechanisms to explain why concentration survives after excluding residual products, why imports can be concentrated without clean same-product export linkage, and why the true action is in scalable corridors.

## What To Avoid

1. Do not claim components broadly explain import concentration. The current evidence weakens that.
2. Do not make the model so flexible that every pattern is explained by an arbitrary fixed cost.
3. Do not rely on exact HS6 import-export matches as the only test of input dependence.
4. Do not call the model "three-country" in the formal title if the model needs many possible partners. Use "three-margin" or "corridor-selection" instead.
5. Do not make welfare claims before adding prices, quantities, and a GE closure.
